Moving a Company's Entire IT After 16 Years With One Supplier

10 November 2026 · Michael Elkær Meisner

Sixteen years is a long time in IT. Long enough for a supplier to stop being a supplier and become part of the furniture. Long enough for knowledge to live in people's heads rather than in documents. And long enough for everyone to assume that changing is impossible.

It is not. But it is not a procurement exercise either.

I led the programme when a global manufacturer moved its entire IT landscape away from a supplier it had worked with for 16 years. Tender, Master Service Agreement, transformation catalogue, portfolio and transition. It was delivered without operational disruption. Looking back, the contract was not what made the difference. People were.

Here is what I learned.

The real risk is not technical

When a board hears “we are changing our IT supplier”, they picture servers, networks and a cut-over weekend. Those are real risks, but they are known risks. There are methods for them.

The risk nobody puts in the risk register is confidence. Around 150 people in the IT organisation were about to have their way of working changed. The board was about to bet on something it had not done for 16 years. If confidence goes, decisions slow down, people protect themselves, and small issues become escalations.

So confidence became a deliverable. Early on I joined a board meeting to explain how the transition would run and why this kind of change is well-known territory. Not to sell it. To make it predictable. Predictability is what a board is really asking for when it asks “is this risky?”

The exiting supplier is your most important partner

This sounds backwards. The supplier is leaving, so why invest in the relationship?

Because the knowledge you need sits with them. Knowledge transfer is the single most important element in a transition, and it depends on the goodwill of people who have just lost the contract.

Don't slam the door. Treat the exiting supplier professionally, respect their constraints and keep the relationship intact. It is the decent thing to do, and it is also the only thing that works. A supplier that feels respected hands over knowledge. A supplier that feels punished hands over documents.

Three parties, one customer

A transition has three parties, not two: the customer, the exiting supplier and the new supplier. The customer sits in the middle and owns the outcome. I call the overlaps Circles of Trust, the places where people from different organisations have to rely on each other to get the work done.

Each overlap needs transparency and open communication, in the formal and the informal channels. Formal governance gives the frame. Informal trust is what makes people pick up the phone before a problem becomes a crisis.

Use the contract as a joint frame, not a weapon

The Master Service Agreement took real effort, and it was worth it. Not because we expected to fight over it, but because a clear contract removes the need to fight. When expectations are written down and shared, both parties can meet problems with open eyes and look for solutions instead of someone to blame.

A contract you only open in a dispute has already failed.

Know who carries the knowledge

Every IT organisation has a handful of people who know how things really work. In a transition they are worth more than any document. Map the key roles early, on both sides, and look after those people through the transition. Lose them, and your plan becomes a guess.

Transformation is a catalogue, not a promise

Moving services is the first step. The value comes after. We built a transformation catalogue and a portfolio from the start, so the new collaboration had a direction from day one and not just a new invoice.

Without it, you risk moving the same problems to a new address.

What I would tell a CIO facing the same decision

  1. Treat confidence as a deliverable, with its own plan and owner.
  2. Invest in the exiting supplier. Knowledge transfer is the transition.
  3. Map your key people before you sign anything.
  4. Write the contract as a frame you will use every week, not a document for the drawer.
  5. Decide what the transformation is before the transition starts.

Sixteen years with one supplier is not a reason to stay. It is a reason to plan the exit with care.
