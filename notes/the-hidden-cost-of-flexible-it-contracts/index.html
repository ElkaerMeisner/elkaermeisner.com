The Hidden Cost of “Flexible” IT Contracts

17 November 2026 · Michael Elkær Meisner

“We need flexibility.” I have heard that sentence in most contract negotiations I have been part of, from customers and from suppliers. It sounds reasonable. Nobody wants to be locked into a scope written before anyone understood the problem.

But flexibility and ambiguity are not the same thing. And ambiguity is expensive.

What flexibility usually means

When a contract is called flexible, it often means the hard questions were not answered. What exactly is included? Who decides when the scope changes? How is a change priced? What happens when the parties disagree about whether something is a change at all?

Leaving those questions open feels efficient at signing. It simply moves the negotiation to later, when the work is running, the deadlines are real and one party has far more leverage than the other.

Where the money goes

The cost of a vague contract rarely shows up as one big number. It shows up as a hundred small conversations.

“It should have been included.” “It is not in the contract.” A change request for something the customer believed was already paid for. An estimate on time and materials that nobody can compare to anything. Weeks spent arguing about the category of a piece of work instead of doing it.

Each conversation is small. Together they eat budget, time and trust. And trust is the most expensive of the three to rebuild.

Real flexibility is designed

Good contracts are flexible, but the flexibility is designed in, not left out. In practice that means four things.

  1. A clear baseline. What is included is described so both parties would explain it the same way to a newcomer.
  2. A boring change mechanism. Everyone knows how a change is raised, assessed, priced and approved, and how long it takes.
  3. Pricing principles agreed upfront. Rates, unit prices or estimation principles are fixed before the first change, not negotiated one change at a time.
  4. A way to disagree. A defined path for the moment the parties see a change differently, with escalation that leads to a decision, not a stalemate.

None of this makes the contract rigid. It makes change predictable. That is what flexibility should mean.

The words that do the damage

Some words appear in almost every contract and almost every dispute: “reasonable”, “best effort”, “market standard”, “as needed”. They feel safe because they sound fair. In practice they postpone the conversation about what fair means.

When I see those words, I ask one question: how would we measure this if we disagreed? If nobody can answer, the word needs a definition, a number or an example.

The contract as a joint frame

I use the contract as the primary tool in a supplier relationship. Not as a weapon, but as a joint frame for the collaboration. That only works when the frame is clear. A vague contract cannot guide anyone. It can only be interpreted, and interpretation is where relationships go wrong.

Clarity is not distrust. It is respect for the people who have to live with the contract long after the negotiators have moved on.

Three questions before you sign

  1. Could a new project manager on either side explain the scope the same way?
  2. Do we know exactly how a change is priced before the first one arrives?
  3. When we disagree, who decides, and by when?

If the answer to any of them is “we will sort it out later”, you have not bought flexibility. You have bought a future negotiation.
