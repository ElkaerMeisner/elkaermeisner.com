Your PMO Is Not a Reporting Office

19 November 2026 · Michael Elkær Meisner

Ask a project manager what the PMO does and you often get a sigh. “They want my status report on Thursday.” Ask the executives and you get a different sigh. “We get plenty of reports, but we still do not know what to stop.”

Both answers point to the same problem. The PMO has become a reporting office.

A reporting office collects. A PMO should help the organisation decide.

The reporting trap

It is easy to see how it happens. A new PMO needs to show value quickly, and reports are visible. Templates are introduced, traffic lights are collected, a monthly pack is produced. Everybody is busy.

But a report nobody acts on is not information. It is overhead. And project managers notice. When reporting is the only thing the PMO asks for, they learn to report what keeps the PMO quiet, not what is actually happening.

What a PMO is for

In the organisations where I have built portfolio management and PMO functions, the useful work came down to three things.

Helping the organisation choose. Which initiatives go ahead, which wait and which stop. That requires a portfolio view, not a list of projects.

Making capacity visible. Most portfolios are not limited by money. They are limited by the same handful of people being needed in every project. Until you can see that, every plan is optimistic.

Making project managers better. Planning, estimation, risk and stakeholder work. A PMO that only inspects does not raise the level. A PMO that teaches does.

Capacity is the honest number

When I implemented portfolio, resource and capacity management for a government agency, the tool was never the point. The point was to put demand and capacity on the same page, so leadership could see the trade-offs instead of guessing at them.

Once that page exists, the conversation changes. It moves from “why are the projects late?” to “what do we stop so the rest can finish?” That is the question a PMO exists to make possible.

Build the capability, not the dependency

In the same engagement I trained the new portfolio manager one to one. Not because training is nice to have, but because a PMO built around a consultant disappears when the consultant leaves.

The same goes for project managers. In a pension company I trained the entire project management department in planning and estimation techniques. Better estimates in the projects mean better decisions in the portfolio. The two are not separate disciplines.

Signs your PMO has become a reporting office

  1. The monthly pack is longer than the list of decisions it led to.
  2. Project managers spend more time on templates than on risks.
  3. Nobody can tell you what the portfolio stopped last quarter.
  4. Capacity is discussed inside projects, never across them.

If two or more sound familiar, it is time to reset the mandate.

Reset in three moves

  1. Give the PMO a decision to support, not a report to produce. Start with the next prioritisation round.
  2. Put demand and capacity on one page, even if the first version is rough.
  3. Cut every report that has not changed a decision in the last three months.

A good PMO is not measured by how much it knows. It is measured by how much better the organisation decides.

What is the one report in your organisation that nobody would miss?
