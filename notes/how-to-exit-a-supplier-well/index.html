Don't Slam the Door: How to Exit a Supplier Well

24 November 2026 · Michael Elkær Meisner

Most organisations spend months choosing a new supplier. Very few spend an afternoon planning how to leave the old one.

That is a mistake. The way you exit a supplier decides how well you start with the next one.

The temptation to punish

When a relationship ends, there is often frustration behind it. Missed expectations, rising costs, a feeling of being taken for granted. It is tempting to let the exit become a final settling of accounts.

Don't. The exiting supplier still runs your services until the last day. Their people still hold knowledge you need. And the market is smaller than you think. You may meet the same people again, on the other side of another table.

Knowledge transfer is the transition

In every transition I have been part of, knowledge transfer has been the single most important element. Systems can be documented. Contracts can be read. But how things actually work, and why, lives with the people who have done the work.

You cannot force that knowledge out with penalty clauses. You can only make it worthwhile and respectful for people to share it.

What a good exit looks like

  1. A clear exit plan, agreed early, with named people on both sides.
  2. A realistic timeline for knowledge transfer, not just a cut-over date.
  3. Respect for the supplier's constraints. They have commercial interests, other customers and people to redeploy.
  4. Honest communication about what happens next, also to the supplier's staff who will be affected.
  5. A formal close that acknowledges the work done, even if the relationship had problems.

The good customer

I often talk about being “the good customer” in a transition. It is not about being soft. It is about acting professionally so suppliers know what to expect from you, whether an engagement is starting or ending.

That reputation matters. Suppliers decide where to put their best people. They put them where they are treated well.

Formal and informal at the same time

A good exit uses the contract as the frame. Exit clauses, obligations and timelines are there for a reason, and both parties should follow them. But the contract does not transfer knowledge. People do.

The formal track keeps everyone safe. The informal track, trust between the people doing the work, is what makes it succeed.

Before you end your next contract

  1. Who on the supplier's side holds knowledge you cannot afford to lose?
  2. What would make it worthwhile for them to share it?
  3. How do you want this supplier to describe you to others in five years?

The door you close today is one you may need to open again.
