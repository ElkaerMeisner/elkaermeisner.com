AI Governance Is Not a New Discipline. It Is an Extension of the One You Already Have

26 November 2026 · Michael Elkær Meisner

Many leaders in regulated organisations face the same challenge right now. They know AI needs governance. They have read the articles, been to the conferences and heard the warnings. And they are left with a feeling that they must invent a whole new discipline from scratch.

They do not.

The most important insight is this: you are not starting with a blank canvas. Most of what you need already exists. Project governance. Vendor management. Risk management. Model validation. AI governance is not a parallel discipline. It is an extension of what the organisation already knows, with three important additions.

Understand the three additions and you have a foundation. The rest is craft.

Why it is urgent

While the perfect policy is being written, three things happen in parallel. Employees use AI tools anyway, without a defined frame, which creates unregistered exposure. Competitors who set up the frame faster gain a productivity lead in low-risk areas, where the gain is largest and the risk smallest. And regulatory exposure grows. The EU AI Act entered into force in August 2024 with obligations applying in phases, and for financial entities DORA has applied since January 2025, with requirements for critical ICT third parties that can, after assessment, include AI services.

The point is not to panic. The point is that the status quo is not neutral. It has a cost too.

Project governance as the backbone

In a classic project you have a steering committee that sets the frame and handles exceptions, a project owner who carries the responsibility, a project manager who drives within the frame, project assurance that assesses independently, and a team that delivers.

The same structure can be reused for every AI use case. The steering committee becomes an AI governance forum, in some organisations an AI council, in others part of the existing risk committee or IT steering group. The name matters less than a named forum with a mandate to decide. The project owner becomes the business owner of the use case. The project manager becomes the use case owner. Assurance becomes risk, compliance and legal. The team becomes the agents.

When you can put named people in those roles for one concrete use case, you have made governance operational for that use case. Not for the whole company. Not for “AI as a phenomenon”. For one use case. That is concrete enough to start, and it is the only level where governance really works.

The three additions

First: the frame must be explicit from day one. In a classic project, tolerances can be adjusted along the way and the project manager uses judgement in the gaps. In AI governance those gaps do not exist in the same way. What is not explicitly allowed or forbidden in your frame, an agent may still attempt. Not out of ill will. It is how it works. So the frame has to be more granular than in a human project. It sounds bureaucratic. It is. That is the price of automation.

Second: institutional accountability cannot be delegated to the AI. When a process is delegated to an agent, the accountability towards customers, regulators and the board stays with the organisation, anchored in the board, executive management and the named business owner of the use case. In practice, validation of output must be proportional to the consequence, not to your trust in the system. And the AI can never be named as responsible when something goes wrong.

Third: the portfolio is managed by risk class, not by budget. The AI Act defines four risk categories, and if you are in scope, that classification belongs at the bottom of your governance. But most internal use cases fall into limited or minimal risk and need a more granular internal taxonomy. A pragmatic three-level model works for most: low (internal productivity, search, drafts), medium (customer communication, classification) and high (decisions with material consequences, automated advice, compliance output). Start in the low-risk class. Not to be cautious, but to learn where a mistake is affordable.

So how do you classify?

The answer is borrowed from agile project management: relative estimation.

Ask an executive team “which risk class is this use case in?” and you get hesitant answers based on gut feeling. Ask “is this use case more or less risky than the one we approved last month?” and you get useful answers. People are reliable at comparing and unreliable at estimating in absolute terms. That is the insight behind planning poker, and it applies here too.

Your first two or three approved use cases become anchor cases. Everything new is classified relative to them on fixed dimensions: data sensitivity, consequence of output, recipient, autonomy, reversibility and regulatory exposure. Diverging votes are not a problem. They are a signal that a factor has been underestimated.

It is not perfect. The first anchor case must be validated independently, or an error spreads through the portfolio. But it works from day one, and it builds on a discipline the organisation already knows.

Three traps

Governance paralysis. Twelve months on a perfect policy before anything is piloted, while people use AI tools anyway. Start with one low-risk use case and build the frame around it.

The tool trap. Choosing the platform before use cases and governance are defined. Governance and use case first, tool last.

Shadow AI. A ban without an alternative. Usage does not disappear. It disappears from logging and audit. Define a policy for permitted use of public AI tools quickly, in parallel with the bigger governance effort.

All three share the same root cause: the belief that governance and speed are opposites. They are not. Clear frames accelerate adoption because they remove uncertainty about what is allowed.

Three questions for your next executive meeting

  1. Who is our first named business owner of an AI use case, and what is the use case?
  2. Which risk class is it in, under the AI Act and in our own taxonomy, and compared to what?
  3. Who owns the frame itself, not the use case but the governance structure?

If you can answer, you have started. If you cannot, that is where the work begins.

Start with one use case. Name an owner. Classify relatively. Learn. Iterate.
