Why Governance Is Not Bureaucracy

12 November 2026 · Michael Elkær Meisner

Say the word governance in a project meeting and watch the room. Eyes drop. Someone thinks of steering committee packs nobody reads. Someone else thinks of approvals that take three weeks.

That is not governance. That is bureaucracy wearing governance's clothes.

Governance is not about control. It is about predictability: who decides what, where, and with which information. When that is clear, things move faster, not slower.

Where bureaucracy comes from

Bureaucracy appears when a structure outlives its purpose. A forum that once solved a real problem keeps meeting after the problem is gone. A report is produced because it has always been produced. An approval step is added after one bad incident and never removed.

Nobody designs bureaucracy. It accumulates.

The cure is not less governance. It is governance where every forum, report and approval can answer one question: what decision does this enable?

Three layers, three different jobs

The model I keep coming back to has three layers. It is simple, but each layer has a distinct job, and most trouble starts when they are mixed.

The strategic layer sets direction. Is the collaboration, programme or supplier still delivering what we need? Where are we heading in the next two to three years? This is for executives, a few times a year, and it should be short.

The tactical layer turns direction into priorities: portfolio, budgets, contract changes and risks that cross teams. This is where most real decisions are made, typically every month.

The operational layer keeps things running: service delivery, incidents, plans and the weekly rhythm. Fast, close to the work, and empowered to act within clear limits.

What goes wrong when the layers mix

When the strategic forum discusses incidents, executives lose interest and the real strategic questions are never asked. When operational teams must escalate every decision, everything slows down and people stop taking ownership. When the tactical layer is missing, the gap between “the board wants this” and “the team does that” is filled with assumptions.

I have seen all three. Usually at the same time.

A board that actually steers

When I established an application management service for a central bank, we set up customer boards for steering and prioritisation. Not as a formality, but because the customer needed a place to say what mattered most this month, and the teams needed a place to hear it.

It worked because the board had one clear job: prioritise. Not review status. Not admire dashboards. Prioritise, so the teams could work without guessing.

That is governance doing its job. It made the work simpler.

Start simple

You do not need a governance manual to get started. You need three things on a single page:

  1. Which forums exist, on which layer, and how often they meet.
  2. Which decisions each forum owns, and which it does not.
  3. When and how an issue moves up a layer.

If a forum cannot point to a decision it owns, close it. If a decision has no forum, you have found your next problem.

The test

Here is a simple test for any governance structure. Ask the people in it: “What would be slower if this did not exist?”

If they can answer, it is governance. If they cannot, it is bureaucracy, and you have just found time to give back to your organisation.

Which forum in your organisation would you close tomorrow if you could?
