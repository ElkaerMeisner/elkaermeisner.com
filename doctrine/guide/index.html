The Doctrine in ten minutes

An agent is a worker without a contract. Everything we know about running workers, suppliers and systems applies to it, and almost none of it is being applied.

This guide follows the book: the argument, the ten rules, the ideas that carry them, the ladder and the board’s part. Each part has something to try on an agent of your own. Your answers stay on this page. Nothing you tick or choose is stored or sent.

03 · The Doctrine

Ten rules in four groups

The ten rules fall into four groups. The Regime is what an organisation runs in order to live by them, and the ladder measures how far it has come. Choose a rule to see what it says and what to ask.

01

Every agent has an owner and a purpose.

One named person answers for it, and one sentence says what it is for. If nobody can be named, the agent does not run.

Under ordinary conditions, silence means the agent continues. Under the Doctrine, silence means the agent comes up for decision.
Questions to ask
  1. Can we name the owner of every agent we run, as a person?
  2. Does every agent have a purpose stated in one sentence?
  3. What happens on the end date, for a small agent and for a large one?
  4. What happens to an agent when its owner changes role or leaves?

Chapter 5, Ownership and the end date. Put it to work with the Agent Register.

03

An agent gets no more access than its job needs.

It has a known identity, and its reach is something someone decided.

The attacker does not break in. The attacker gets text in front of the agent.
Questions to ask
  1. For each of our large agents, which of the three identities does it use?
  2. Which agents combine private data, outside content and a way to act or send?
  3. When were the permissions of our largest agents last reviewed?
  4. Who approves a new tool or connection for an agent?

Chapter 6, Access, and the agent that can be talked into things. Try it under Access.

04

The human moves up the stack.

People set the limits, receive the exceptions and take the decisions an agent may not. The limits are held outside the agent.

The human has not left the loop. The human has moved up the stack, from doing the work to governing it.
Questions to ask
  1. For each important agent, are the tolerances written down with numbers or rules?
  2. Is each limit held by something outside the agent?
  3. Where a person approves, how often do they change what the agent proposed?
  4. Which decisions are being made on agent output without anyone having chosen that?

Chapter 7, The human moves up the stack. Try it under Limits.

05

Control follows size.

How much governance an agent gets depends on what it can reach and what happens when it is wrong.

The Doctrine does not ask anyone to prefer judgment to data. It asks them not to wait for data they do not have.
Questions to ask
  1. Have we sized our agents, or are they all treated the same?
  2. Have we written the floors, and do they follow our risk appetite?
  3. Is the legal classification recorded separately from our own size?
  4. When did we last sort the list again in light of what has happened?

Chapter 8, Control follows size. Try it under Size.

06

Nothing runs without a record.

If the question “how many agents do we have, and what can they do” takes more than a day to answer, there is no governance yet.

How many agents do you have? If the answer takes more than a day, the organisation does not have governance. It has hope.
Questions to ask
  1. Could we answer “how many agents, and what can they do” within one working day?
  2. Can we see which data sources and processes each agent is connected to?
  3. Who is responsible for the class of personal assistants?
  4. Is there any way for an agent to go live without a record?

07

Proof before trust.

A demonstration is a handful of observations. Evidence is many cases with known answers, run again after every change.

A demonstration is five observations. Proof is hundreds of cases with known answers, run again after every change.
Questions to ask
  1. How do we know how our largest agents behave, beyond having seen them work?
  2. When was that evidence produced, and against which model version?
  3. Would we find out if a vendor replaced the model under one of them?
  4. How many changes did we find last quarter, as opposed to approve?

08

Change is a decision, not an event.

New data, new permissions, a new model or a new purpose are decided by someone, or found and tested when they arrive from outside.

09

An agent can fail, be stopped and be accounted for.

Incidents are recorded, a named person can stop it, and a log shows what it did.

A log does for an agent what minutes do for a meeting. Without one, what the agent did is an anecdote.
Questions to ask
  1. Have we defined what an agent incident is, and has one ever been recorded?
  2. Who can stop each large agent, also at night, and has the stop been tried?
  3. Does each agent keep a log, and does anyone read it?
  4. Could we run our most important agent-supported process without the agent?

Chapter 11, When it goes wrong. Try it under Incidents, and put it to work with the Incident and Stop Log.

10

A vendor’s agent is a vendor relationship.

Data, scope, price, model changes, liability and exit are settled in writing before the agent matters.

Almost no agent is free of suppliers. In most cases the organisation has negotiated with none of them about agents.
Questions to ask
  1. For each agent, which suppliers does it depend on, including the model provider?
  2. Have we read the terms that actually govern the agent features we use?
  3. What is included in the price, and what is the ceiling?
  4. Would we be told before a model is replaced, and who pays for testing again?
  5. Could we move our most important agent, and what would it take?

04 · Rule 03 in practice

Three things that should not meet

The risk is highest when three things come together in one agent: access to private data, exposure to content from outside, and a way to send something out or to act. Each alone is manageable.

Start from an agent in the book

Or tick what your own agent has.

Whose identity is it using?

An agent acts under one of three identities, and the difference decides most of its risk.

Its own

It has a service identity with permissions granted to it. Its reach can be reviewed and withdrawn.

Its builder’s

It runs with the credentials of the person who made it. Everyone who uses it borrows that person’s access.

Its user’s

It acts with the access of whoever is talking to it, including everything that was shared too widely years ago.

From chapter 6. An agent of any consequence has its own identity. It gets what its purpose sentence requires and no more.

05 · Rule 04 in practice

A limit holds when it sits outside the agent

Humans do not supervise agents keystroke by keystroke. They set the limits, receive the exceptions and take the decisions the agent is not allowed to take.

Six tolerances for an agent
  • PurposeWhat kind of task is outside its job?
  • DataWhat may it read, and which of its sources are untrusted?
  • ActionWhat may it do alone, and what may it only propose?
  • ValueAbove what amount or consequence must a person decide?
  • VolumeHow much activity is normal, and what level is a warning?
  • EscalationWhich conditions send a case to a person?

Each needs a number or a rule. “Low-value transactions” is an adjective. “Below 5,000, and never to a new recipient” is a tolerance.

07 · Rule 09 in practice

An agent can have an incident

Most organisations have never had an agent incident, because they have never defined one. That is not the same as being safe. The Doctrine defines five.

  • Out of purposeThe agent did something its purpose sentence does not cover.
  • Out of boundsIt reached data or used a permission it should not have.
  • Wrong and relied uponIts output was incorrect and someone acted on it.
  • Silent failureIt stopped or degraded, and nobody noticed.
  • ManipulatedSomeone steered it through its input.

08 · The Regime

The ladder

Compliance is a photograph taken for someone else. Maturity is a position you hold for yourself. The ladder has five levels, each reached by a test, and it is measured on top of legal compliance, never in place of it.

1 Unknown

What is true
The organisation cannot say how many agents it has.
The test
None. It is the starting point.
What it means
Many organisations will find themselves here. That is not a criticism. It is where every organisation stood with every new technology before it decided to look.

2 Listed

What is true
There is a complete and current list of them.
The test
Reconcile with the platforms, both ways.
What it buys
It changes the conversation with outsiders. An organisation that can produce its list on request is treated differently by auditors, supervisors and customers.
What the step takes
Detective work. It takes weeks, and it needs no decisions from anyone senior.

3 Owned

What is true
Every agent has an owner, a purpose, an end date and a size.
The test
A sample of entries and acceptances.
What it buys
It removes the orphans. From here on, agents come up for decision on their own, and somebody can always be called.
What the step takes
Conversation. Someone has to sit down with a manager for each agent that matters and ask whether they will answer for it.

4 Controlled

What is true
Limits, tests, change control, incident handling and logs are operating.
The test
A year of records for the large agents.
What it buys
The organisation can survive an incident. It will know what happened, be able to stop it, and be able to show afterwards what it did.
What the step takes
The heavy one. Limits, tests, logs, change control and a stop that works have to be built for the large agents.

5 Assured

What is true
The level is confirmed independently, suppliers are covered, and findings lead to decisions.
The test
Audit review and supplier terms.
What it buys
The board can rely on what it is told, because someone who does not run the agents has checked.
What the step takes
Independence. The work is modest. What makes it hard is that it invites findings.

Not every organisation needs level 5. The target should follow what the agents can reach. Choosing it is a decision about risk appetite, and it belongs to the board and to the minutes.

Three ways to fool yourself

Skip levels

Starting at level 4, because change control and incident processes feel like real governance. Without a complete list and real owners, they have nothing to act on.

Round up

Reporting the level that is almost reached. “We are essentially at level 3” means level 2. The levels are discrete, so a level means that the test was passed.

Report the best area

Finance may be on level 3 while marketing is on level 1. The honest report gives the lowest level among the areas that matter and shows the spread.

09 · For the board and executive management

What the board answers for

A board does not need to learn how language models work. It needs enough knowledge to judge the risk, to set an appetite for it and to tell a good answer from a poor one.

Four things to know about agents

  1. 1They act with the organisation’s access.
  2. 2Their behaviour varies.
  3. 3They can be steered by what they read.
  4. 4They change when their suppliers change them.

With that, a board member can hear the difference between “we have tested it” and “we ran three hundred known cases last month and it passed 96 percent of them”.

Three decisions that are the board’s

The board is being asked to do three things, and to refuse to do the rest.

1 The appetite for delegation

How much the organisation is willing to delegate to software. That is a decision about risk appetite, and the appetite is the board’s.

2 The level on the ladder

The level the organisation should reach on the ladder, and by when.

3 Agents in critical functions

The approval of each agent that acts on its own in a critical function, if the appetite reserves that for the board.

Everything else belongs to management: which agents to build, who owns them, what their limits are, how they are tested.

An appetite for delegation

A board that has approved a risk appetite for operational and technology risk has most of what it needs. What is usually missing is a statement about delegation to software. It could read like this:

We accept agents that draft and propose in all functions. We accept agents that act on their own only where the action can be reversed, the value is below a limit we have set, and a named person can stop the agent. We do not accept agents that act on their own in critical functions without our approval of each.

The wording will differ from one organisation to the next. What matters is that it exists and that it is the board’s.

Ten questions

These are the questions a board can put to executive management at its next meeting. A weak answer is a department where a name was asked for, a promise where a number was asked for, or the assurance that there is a human in the loop. A good answer has a name, a date and something that can be shown.

Tick each question that got a good answer

Hear the difference

How many agents do we have, and how do we know the list is complete?

A weak answer

“IT has a good overview, and we are working on a complete inventory as part of the AI programme.”

A good answer

“Sixty-three as of the end of October. We compared the record with what the three platforms show. Five were running that we had not recorded. Four of those are now recorded and one has been switched off.”

The second answer is not good because the news is comfortable. It is good because it has a count, a date, a method and a difference, and because each of them could be checked by someone who doubted it.

Who can stop them, also at night, when was that last tried, and how is the work done while they are stopped?

A weak answer

A weak answer names the IT department.

A good answer

A good answer names two people for each large agent and says that the stop for the complaint agent was tried in September and took nine minutes. It also says how complaints are handled while the agent is stopped.

Asking is oversight. Answering is management.

  2. Agents by size, with change since last quarter

  4. Agents past their end date, by name
  5. Changes approved and changes found
  6. Incidents and near misses, and what changed afterwards

What waiting costs

Three situations show the cost, and none of them appears in a budget.

The inventory does not exist

A supervisor or an auditor asks for the inventory of agents and it does not exist. A team spends weeks reconstructing it, and the organisation is asked more questions afterwards than it would have been otherwise.

Wrong and relied upon for a month

An agent is wrong and relied upon for a month before anyone notices. The organisation has to find every case, contact the people affected and explain why nobody was checking.

The terms are silent

A supplier changes a model or a price, and the terms turn out to be silent. The organisation pays, because the alternative is to rebuild.

10 · Where to start

The first ninety days

The first round covers agents that have their own access or act on their own. Before day 1, three decisions are needed from executive management: who coordinates, which existing forum takes agent decisions, and the terms of the amnesty.

  1. 30Days 1 to 30. Inventory from all sources. Amnesty for unregistered agents. Existing incident duties apply from day one. Result: a first list.
  2. 60Days 31 to 60. Owner, purpose, end date and size for every agent in scope. Floors agreed with the risk function. Result: level 3 for agents in scope.
  3. 90Days 61 to 90. Identity and limits for large agents. Stop holders named. First page to the board. Result: a basis for the board’s ten questions.
